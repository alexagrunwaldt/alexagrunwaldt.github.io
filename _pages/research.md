<p>My research follows families through major life events. When someone is diagnosed with dementia, what happens to their household's work and earnings? ## Job market paper

### [The Economic Consequences of Alzheimer's and Dementia: Evidence from Patients and Households in Colombia](/files/jobmarketpaper.pdf)

<p class="meta">with Christian Posso and Andrea Franco Hernandez</p>

Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD) can reduce a patient's ability to work while increasing care needs at home. We study how formal employment and earnings change for patients and their household members in Colombia, where formal long-term care is limited and multigenerational living is common. Linking national health records to monthly formal-employment data and SISBEN household rosters, we estimate stacked difference-in-differences event studies comparing newly diagnosed patients and their households with matched, never-diagnosed comparisons. Two years after a first recorded diagnosis, working-age patients' formal employment is about 15 percentage points lower. Among other working-age household members, formal employment is 3.5 percentage points lower after one year and 4.5 points lower after two, and earnings decline for both women and men, providing no evidence that relatives offset the patient's losses through their own formal work. These results document household-level losses in formal-sector attachment that patient-level analyses miss.

## Working papers

### [Homelessness and the Persistence of Deprivation: Income, Employment, and Safety Net Participation](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32323)

<p class="meta">with Bruce D. Meyer, Angela Wyse, Gillian Meyer, and Derek Wu<br>NBER Working Paper 32323, 2024</p>

Homelessness is arguably the most extreme hardship associated with poverty in the United States, yet people experiencing homelessness are excluded from official poverty statistics. This paper provides the first national estimates of their income, employment, and safety net participation based on administrative data. Starting from the first large and nationally representative sample of adults recorded as sheltered and unsheltered homeless, taken from the 2010 Census, we link restricted-use longitudinal tax records and administrative data on safety net programs, housing assistance, and mortality. Nearly half of these adults were employed in the year they were observed as homeless, and nearly all either worked or were reached by at least one safety net program. Nevertheless, their incomes remained low for the decade surrounding an observed period of homelessness, suggesting that homelessness tends to arise in the context of long-term, severe deprivation rather than large and sudden losses of income.

### State Income Taxes and Retiree Migration: Evidence from Household-Specific Tax Incentives

States compete for retirees through tax preferences for Social Security, pensions, and other retirement income, but it is unclear whether these policies induce retirees to move or only shape where movers settle. Using CPS ASEC data from 1982–2019 and TAXSIM, I calculate the after-tax income each retiree household would receive in every state given its own mix of income, and estimate conditional logit models of location choice. Among retirees overall, after-tax income has little effect on where they live, and distance from the origin state is a much stronger predictor. Among interstate movers, however, retirees are significantly more likely to choose states where they would receive higher after-tax income. State tax policy appears to do little to induce retirees to relocate, but it can shape competition for those who have already decided to move.

### Household Criminality and the Intergenerational Transmission of Disadvantage: Evidence from Medellín, Colombia

<p class="meta">with Christian Posso</p>

This paper examines how children's outcomes change when an adult who becomes involved in crime enters their household. Using linked household survey, education, and crime records from Medellín, we compare children who experience the arrival of such an adult with children who experience the arrival of an adult who does not commit a crime. Children exposed to a criminally involved adult are 9–10 percentage points more likely to drop out of school and 1–1.3 percentage points more likely to have a recorded criminal offense, and those who take the high-school mathematics exam score 0.05–0.06 standard deviations lower. The estimates are similar after matching on child, household, and mover characteristics.
